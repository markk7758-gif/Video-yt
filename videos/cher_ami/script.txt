A pigeon with one eye, one leg and a hole in its chest saved almost two hundred soldiers. Maybe.

It's October 1918. Five hundred American soldiers are trapped in a French forest, surrounded by Germans. No radio. No way out.

Then it gets worse. Their own artillery starts shelling them.

They have one way to call for help: pigeons. The first one takes off. The Germans shoot it down. The second one. Shot down.

They have one pigeon left. His name is Cher Ami. He carries a tiny note: "For heaven's sake, stop it."

He's shot almost immediately. He falls. Then, somehow, he gets back up and keeps flying.

Wait. How does a bleeding pigeon find its way home?

You see, homing pigeons always fly back to their loft, like you finding your bed in the dark. Nothing stops them, not even bullets.

Twenty-five minutes later, he lands. Blind in one eye, one leg hanging by a thread. The guns stop. 194 men walk out alive.

France gives him a war medal. The Army gives him a wooden leg.

And historians? They're still arguing whether he really did it.

But in 2021, scientists did prove one thing. The Army called Cher Ami a girl. He was a boy.
